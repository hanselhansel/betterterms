# Counterparties: who decides a promotion case

Patterns by decision-maker, not facts. Every company runs its own
process; confirm the actual path at intake ("who decides") and during
research before relying on a pattern below.

## The direct manager

- Usually the advocate, rarely the decider. They take the case
  upward, so give them something they can forward: a short written
  case with the impact numbers, the market data, and the ask.
- Their predictable objections are the rehearsal set: budget is set,
  not the right time, you are already at the top of the band, let me
  check with the committee.
- A supportive manager who cannot say yes still matters: ask them
  what the decider needs to see, and put it in the case.

## The promotion committee or calibration round

- Decides on evidence packets, not on the meeting. The written
  impact file does the work; the manager reads it into the record.
- Level expectations matter more than feelings here: map each impact
  item to the written expectations of the target level, if the user
  has or can get the leveling guide.
- Decisions come in batches on a fixed calendar. A well-timed
  well-documented ask beats a brilliant off-cycle one.

## The skip-level or department head

- Decides or sponsors where there is no formal committee. Cares
  about org outcomes and budget, so tie the impact file to team
  results, not personal effort.
- Often owns the headcount and level slots the manager cannot see.
  A "no slot" answer from them is real; a "no slot" answer relayed
  through the manager is worth one polite probe.

## HR and the compensation team

- Own the bands, the budget, and the process rules. "Top of band"
  and "no budget" answers live here.
- They can state the pay scale for a level in writing; in some
  states and countries the user has a right to it. See
  `references/rights.md`.
- HR enforces the calendar: mid-cycle asks are the exception path
  and usually need a decider's sponsorship.

## Outside recruiters

- Real outside interest is market data and the strongest card in
  the ask. A recruiter conversation is not an offer; only a written
  offer plays as an outside offer.
- If the user would not take the outside job, do not script it as a
  threat. See playbook move 8.

## Who you reach

- The direct manager first, in a dedicated meeting the user
  schedules for the purpose, not squeezed into a standing one on
  one. From there the ask travels to whoever decides.
- The strongest artifact is the shortest: one page the manager can
  forward unchanged. Numbers, level mapping, the ask, the options.
